Conditions

The 2006 Consensus Statement grouped disorders of sex development (DSD) into sex chromosome DSD, 46,XY DSD, and 46,XX DSD.[1] Each condition below is distinct. Grouping them under one label can hide very different needs.

  • Klinefelter syndrome
  • 48,XXXY and 49,XXXXY
  • Turner syndrome
  • Complete androgen insensitivity (CAIS)
  • Partial androgen insensitivity (PAIS)
  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH, 21-hydroxylase deficiency)
  • 5-alpha-reductase deficiency
  • 17β-HSD3 deficiency
  • Ovotesticular DSD
  • Mixed gonadal dysgenesis
  • Swyer syndrome (46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis)
  • 46,XX testicular DSD

Klinefelter syndrome

Karyotype
47,XXY
Biology
An extra X chromosome in people with a Y chromosome. The testes are small and produce less testosterone.[6]
Typical presentation
Male development. Often diagnosed at puberty or during infertility evaluation: small testes, reduced testosterone, possible breast development, taller stature, and learning or language differences in some people.[6]
Prevalence
About 1 in 650 male newborns. It is the most common sex chromosome disorder.[6]
Health needs
Testosterone therapy, fertility counselling, and educational support. Many people are never diagnosed.

48,XXXY and 49,XXXXY

Karyotype
48,XXXY / 49,XXXXY
Biology
Two or three extra X chromosomes alongside a Y. Effects are generally more pronounced than in 47,XXY.[7][8]
Typical presentation
Boys and men with small testes and low testosterone. Developmental, speech, and learning differences are common, and 49,XXXXY often involves skeletal and heart differences.
Prevalence
48,XXXY: 1 in 17,000 to 1 in 50,000 newborn boys.[7] 49,XXXXY: about 1 in 85,000 to 100,000 babies born with a Y chromosome.[8]
Health needs
Hormone therapy, developmental therapies, and multidisciplinary care.

Turner syndrome

Karyotype
45,X (also mosaic forms)
Biology
Complete or partial loss of one X chromosome.[9]
Typical presentation
Female development, short stature, and ovarian insufficiency (usually no spontaneous puberty). Some people have heart or kidney differences.
Prevalence
About 1 in 2,000 female newborns, and much more common among miscarriages.[9]
Health needs
Growth hormone, estrogen replacement, cardiac monitoring, and fertility counselling.

Complete androgen insensitivity (CAIS)

Karyotype
46,XY
Biology
Variants in the AR gene mean the body's cells cannot respond to androgens (testosterone). Testes form and make testosterone, but the body develops along female lines.[10]
Typical presentation
Typical female external anatomy. There is no uterus, and the testes are internal. Often diagnosed when periods don't start, or when an inguinal hernia is found in childhood.
Prevalence
2 to 5 in 100,000 newborn females.[10]
Health needs
Decisions about gonad removal and its timing, hormone replacement if the gonads are removed, bone health, and psychological support.

Partial androgen insensitivity (PAIS)

Karyotype
46,XY
Biology
Partial response to androgens because of AR variants.[10]
Typical presentation
Wide range of genital appearance, from mostly female to mostly male, including atypical genitalia.
Prevalence
Thought to be at least as common as CAIS.[10]
Health needs
Individualized care. Sex of rearing and any surgery are among the most debated questions (see debated questions).

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH, 21-hydroxylase deficiency)

Karyotype
Usually 46,XX or 46,XY
Biology
An enzyme deficiency reduces cortisol (and often aldosterone) production, and the adrenal glands overproduce androgens.[11]
Typical presentation
Classic salt-wasting CAH can be life-threatening in newborns. 46,XX infants may have atypical genitalia but have ovaries and a uterus. Non-classic forms appear later.
Prevalence
Classic: about 1 in 15,000 newborns. Non-classic: about 1 in 1,000.[11]
Health needs
Lifelong steroid replacement, adrenal crisis prevention, and newborn screening. Support: CARES Foundation.[21]

5-alpha-reductase deficiency

Karyotype
46,XY
Biology
SRD5A2 variants reduce conversion of testosterone to DHT, which is needed for typical male external genital development before birth.[12]
Typical presentation
At birth the genitals may look female or atypical. Masculinization often occurs at puberty, and some people raised as girls change to a male gender role.
Prevalence
Rare, and exact incidence unknown. Clusters have been found in the Dominican Republic, Papua New Guinea, Turkey, and Egypt.[12]
Health needs
Specialist assessment, informed choices about sex of rearing, and fertility support.

17β-HSD3 deficiency

Karyotype
46,XY
Biology
HSD17B3 variants impair testosterone production in the testes.[13]
Typical presentation
Often female-appearing at birth, with virilization at puberty.
Prevalence
About 1 in 147,000 newborns, and higher in some populations (1 in 200 to 300 in the Arab population of Gaza).[13]
Health needs
Similar to 5-ARD: specialist care and shared decision-making.

Ovotesticular DSD

Karyotype
46,XX most often; also 46,XY or mosaic
Biology
Both ovarian and testicular tissue are present, either in one gonad (an ovotestis) or as one ovary and one testis.[16]
Typical presentation
External genitalia may be ambiguous, or may look female or male. The cause is usually unknown.[16]
Prevalence
Rare. We could not verify a reliable population prevalence from an authoritative source. Genetic causes are under study.[18]
Health needs
Gonadal assessment, tumor surveillance, and individualized care.

Mixed gonadal dysgenesis

Karyotype
Usually 45,X/46,XY mosaic
Biology
Asymmetric gonads, typically a testis on one side and an underdeveloped 'streak' gonad on the other.[17]
Typical presentation
Broad range, from female-appearing to male-appearing genitals. Some people have features of Turner syndrome.
Prevalence
Rare. Prevalence figure not verified for this draft.[17]
Health needs
Gonadal tumor risk monitoring, growth, hormone, and fertility care.

Swyer syndrome (46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis)

Karyotype
46,XY
Biology
The gonads do not develop into testes (they remain 'streak' gonads). About 15% of cases involve SRY variants.[14]
Typical presentation
Female anatomy with a uterus. Puberty does not start without treatment.
Prevalence
About 1 in 80,000 people.[14]
Health needs
Estrogen/progesterone therapy, removal of streak gonads because of high tumor risk, and pregnancy via donor eggs is possible.

46,XX testicular DSD

Karyotype
46,XX
Biology
Testes develop despite an XX karyotype, most often because the SRY gene has moved onto an X chromosome.[15]
Typical presentation
Usually male appearance. Small testes, infertility, and sometimes atypical genitalia.
Prevalence
About 1 in 20,000 individuals with a male appearance.[15]
Health needs
Testosterone therapy and fertility counselling.
