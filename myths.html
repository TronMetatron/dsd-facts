Myths vs facts

These are common claims, with what the medical evidence says.

Myth: Intersex people have "two full sets of genitals."

Fact: No DSD produces two complete, functioning sets of external genitals. The closest condition is ovotesticular DSD, which involves gonadal tissue (ovarian and testicular), either in one ovotestis or as one ovary and one testis. External genitals may be ambiguous, or may look typically female or male.[16]

Myth: "1.7% of people are intersex."

Fact: The 1.7% figure comes from Blackless, Fausto-Sterling and colleagues (2000).[3] It counts a broad set of conditions, including Klinefelter syndrome, Turner syndrome, and late-onset (non-classic) CAH. Sax (2002) argued that most clinicians don't classify these as intersex. Restricting the term to cases where chromosomal sex is inconsistent with phenotypic sex, or the phenotype is not classifiable as male or female, gives about 0.018%, "almost 100 times lower."[4] The difference comes mostly from definitions, so any prevalence figure should state which conditions it includes. Fausto-Sterling's reply to critics is also published.[5]

Myth: XXY means a person is "both sexes."

Fact: 47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome) is a sex chromosome variation in people with a Y chromosome, who develop testes and male anatomy. The main effects are lower testosterone and infertility.[6] Most clinicians do not consider it intersex in the sense of ambiguous anatomy.[4] The same applies to 48,XXXY and 49,XXXXY, which affect boys and men.[7][8]

Myth: "Androgen sensitivity" makes someone male and female.

Fact: The condition is androgen insensitivity. Because of AR gene variants, the body's cells can't respond (fully or partly) to testosterone. In the complete form, people with 46,XY chromosomes and internal testes develop typical female external anatomy and usually live as women.[10]

Genuinely debated questions

"Intersex" vs "DSD" terminology

The 2006 Consensus introduced "disorders of sex development" to replace terms like "hermaphrodite" and "pseudohermaphrodite."[1] Some patients and advocates prefer "differences of sex development" or "intersex," and others prefer the specific diagnosis name. This site uses "DSD" and the condition name, and respects each person's preferred language for themselves.

Timing of genital surgery

The 2006 Consensus called for care by multidisciplinary teams, open communication, and caution about surgery.[1] The 2016 global update, which included support and advocacy group representatives, states that decisions about gender assignment and "surgery during infancy that alters external genital anatomy or removes germ cells continue to carry risk."[2] Clinicians, parents, and adults with DSD hold differing views on timing, so we present this as an open question.
